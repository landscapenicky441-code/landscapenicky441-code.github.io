Hunter, Not Scanner — A Methodology for Authorized Bug Bounty

Created2026-05-20|Methodology

Most people who plateau in bug bounty plateau for the same reason: they behave like a scanner. They reach for the most template-able primitive, find one access-control issue, and stop. The deep bugs — business logic, chained privilege escalation, race conditions, server-side injection — stay invisible because they were never looking at the target, only at it. This is the methodology I use to hunt instead of scan, inside strict authorization boundaries.

Model the target before you touch a payload

The single highest-leverage habit: understand the target before attacking it. Before any request, answer:

  • Actors — anonymous, user, premium, merchant, support, admin, API client, other tenants. What can each do?
  • Valuable assets — PII, money/points/coupons, files, admin functions, other users’ data, internal services. Where’s the value?
  • State machine — register → verify → pay → fulfil; order → settle → ship; refund; withdraw. What changes at each step?
  • Trust boundaries — client↔server, service↔service, tenant↔tenant, front-end vs back-end validation. Where do the two sides disagree?
  • Tech-stack fingerprint — framework, language, middleware. This decides which injection/deserialization classes are even physically possible.

Only now do you form hypotheses — and they must span classes. If 80% of your hypotheses are “access control,” you don’t understand the target yet. Force yourself: is there a race here? a logic skip? where does this parameter flow (injection / SSRF)? can this multi-step flow be jumped?

The floor is not the ceiling

An access-control bug is a floor, not a ceiling. When you find one, don’t stop — ask “what does this unlock, and how far up the severity ladder does it chain?”

IDOR and logic bugs are the middle. Standing in a business API, the first question is “can this RCE / can this take over an account?”, not “can this IDOR?”. Injection → RCE, deserialization → RCE, upload → webshell, SSRF → metadata → takeover. Chase the top of the ladder; report the top.

Depth vs. breadth — and the redline

Two axes people conflate:

  • Depth = arguing severity. No ceiling. From one unauthorized read, argue “IDs enumerate → whole table readable → enumerate to an admin → takeover” — using two or three of your own test accounts to prove the pattern.
  • Breadth = real extraction. Hard-capped by the rules. Prove reachability; never take the whole table.

That distinction is the entire ethics of the job. You demonstrate impact by argument and minimal proof, not by mass extraction.

Proof, not attack surface

The bar for a report is strict: demonstrated harm, not demonstrated existence.

  • “The endpoint is reachable” / “the CORS header reflects” / “an ID enumerates” — that’s attack surface, not a vulnerability.
  • A finding qualifies when harm is proven, not inferred: you actually read another user’s record, actually moved a value, actually executed. Delete the “if… then could…” language.

One proven, qualifying bug beats nine attack-surface notes. Programs pay for demonstrated impact.

The rules I work under

Everything above happens inside hard boundaries, because that’s what separates a researcher from an attacker:

  • In-scope only — a formal program or written authorization.
  • Minimal, non-destructive PoC — at most a few sample records to prove reachability; injection stops at version(), RCE stops at whoami.
  • No mass extraction, no DoS, no ransomware, no out-of-scope targeting.
  • PII redacted in every artifact; every state-changing action logged and reverted where possible.

Reporting

A good report is short and reproducible: the endpoint and parameter, the minimal PoC, the demonstrated impact, and a concrete fix. Severity is reasoned from the endpoint of the attack — what the attacker actually holds — not from how long or clever the chain was. A five-step chain that ends in non-sensitive data is not a critical; a one-step read of another user’s identity document is.

That’s the whole game: understand the target, form cross-class hypotheses, chain to the top of the severity ladder in argument, prove the minimum, and report it cleanly — all strictly within authorization.
